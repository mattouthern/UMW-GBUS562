# Finding the Right Spot for Wildframe's Pop-Up

Before picking a winner, let's think through what actually matters here: foot traffic of the *right* people, cost per week, visibility, and whether the location fits the brand (warm, a little irreverent, eco-conscious urban professionals who care about quality).

## Budget Reality Check

$5,000 over 4 weeks = **$1,250/week**. That rules out prime mall kiosks or flagship retail corridors, but it's workable for farmers markets, transit-adjacent spots, or shared commercial space.

## Options Worth Weighing

**1. Farmers Market Stall**

* *Cost:* Usually $150–$400/week — leaves budget room for a nicer booth setup, signage, or samples.
* *Pros:* Built-in audience of people who already care about sourcing, quality, and sustainability. Low commitment, easy to test messaging.
* *Cons:* Limited hours (often weekends only), smaller daily reach.

**2. Business District Sidewalk Cart (near offices, not malls)**

* *Cost:* Permit fees vary by city, often $200–$600/week depending on location.
* *Pros:* Direct line to the "urban professional" commuter — morning coffee run is prime real estate for a subscription pitch.
* *Cons:* Weather-dependent, may need insurance/permits that eat into budget.

**3. Shared Retail Space / Co-working Lobby**

* *Cost:* $800–$1,200/week depending on neighborhood.
* *Pros:* Built-in indoor traffic, protected from weather, often already filled with your target demo (remote workers, freelancers, young professionals).
* *Cons:* Less "discovery" traffic — you're mostly reaching people who already work there.

**4. Transit Hub Kiosk (train station, ferry terminal)**

* *Cost:* Can run $1,000–$1,500/week — tight but possible.
* *Pros:*\* Massive volume, habitual coffee-buying moment, great for brand awareness.
* *Cons:* Less time for conversation/education — people are rushing, so it's more transactional than relationship-building.

## Our Recommendation: **Split the Difference**

Rather than one location for all four weeks, consider **rotating between 2 spots**:

* **Weeks 1–2:** Farmers market (cheap, high-affinity audience, great for feedback and face time)
* **Weeks 3–4:** Business district sidewalk or co-working lobby (introduces Wildframe to people with less time but more frequent purchasing power)

This keeps you under budget, tests two different customer touchpoints, and gives you real data on where your subscription sign-ups actually come from — without blowing the whole budget on one bet.

**Bottom line:** Don't chase the busiest spot — chase the *right* bodies. A farmers market full of people who bring their own tote bags is worth more to Wildframe than a train station full of people grabbing the nearest cup on autopilot.

# Working Through the Numbers

**Union Station is out before we even look at sales.** Doesn't matter how good the traffic is if we can't afford to be there for the full four weeks.

## Step 2: Revenue \& Profit (Riverside vs. Campus)

**Riverside Farmers Market**

* Buyers/week: 9,000 × 2% = 180
* Weekly revenue: 180 × $14 = $2,520
* Weekly profit: $2,520 − $900 = **$1,620**
* 4-week profit: **$6,480**

**Campus Library Atrium**

* Buyers/week: 6,000 × 3% = 180
* Weekly revenue: 180 × $5 = $900
* Weekly profit: $900 − $600 = **$300**
* 4-week profit: **$1,200**

Even though both locations net the same number of buyers per week (180), Riverside generates over **5x the profit** because of higher basket size.

## Step 3: Look Past Revenue — Brand Fit \& Subscriptions

This is where the decision really sharpens:

* **Product mix matters.** Riverside sells whole-bean bags — people buying beans at home are already acting like subscribers-in-training. Campus and Union Station sell single cups, which are impulse buys, not relationship-starters.
* **Audience match matters.** Riverside's farmers market crowd overlaps directly with "environmentally conscious urban professionals" — our actual target. Campus traffic is mostly students, who are a weaker fit for a $20+/month bean subscription. Union Station is commuters grabbing whatever's fastest — high volume, low intent.
* **Leftover budget matters.** Riverside leaves $1,400 unspent — enough for samples, subscription sign-up cards, or a QR code offer to convert buyers into subscribers on the spot.

## Step 4: Sanity Check on Union Station

Worth noting: even if we *could* afford Union Station, it operates at a **weekly loss** (-$1,100/week, or -$4,400 over 4 weeks) due to low conversion and low ticket size relative to rent. High foot traffic sounds appealing, but the math doesn't support it as a standalone strategy at this rent level.

## Bottom Line

Riverside Farmers Market wins on all three fronts: it's the only location that's both financially sound *and* fits the brand's actual customer and conversion goals. Campus is profitable but small-scale and demographically mismatched. Union Station is disqualified by budget and would lose money even if the rent fit.
